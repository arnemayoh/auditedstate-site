Interpretive Entropy: Orientation Lost, Fatigue Felt

Why knowledge systems need reasoning memory

A civilization becomes tired when understanding no longer accumulates. — Arne Mayoh

Across many societies, a subtle exhaustion has taken hold. Information flows continuously, conversations multiply, and yet more people report feeling less oriented than before — not less informed, less oriented. That's a specific, different complaint, and it points at something more precise than ordinary information overload.

Orientation is the ability to locate yourself within a landscape of ideas. Understanding a field takes more than access to information — it takes access to the reasoning that actually produced that information. Historically, durable reasoning artifacts held this up: scientific literature, legal archives, institutional records, all of it preserving reasoning trajectories well enough that knowledge could genuinely accumulate rather than just pile up. The fatigue many people feel now and the orientation many people have lost are not two separate problems. They're the felt and the structural sides of the same one: interpretive entropy — the divergence between an idea's original meaning and how it gets interpreted later, as it moves through systems never built to preserve that meaning.

Noise Without Trajectory

Modern communication environments generate continuous discourse — ideas circulate fast through posts, threads, and commentary streams, but most of it leaves little durable reasoning structure behind. Arguments restart rather than continue. Interpretations drift. The same debates repeat, not because anyone's being obstinate, but because nothing carried the previous round's actual reasoning forward to build on. Reasoning becomes fast and, in the same motion, forgetful. That fragmentation is exactly what raises reconstruction cost — participants keep having to rebuild arguments from fragments instead of just continuing them.

Why This Produces Fatigue, Not Just Confusion

When reasoning keeps restarting instead of continuing, people encounter the same debates again and again — and the fatigue that produces is not simple information overload. It's the specific exhaustion of discourse that never stabilizes into anything you could call continuing understanding. Confusion is what you feel when you don't have enough information. This is different: it's what you feel when you have plenty of information and still can't locate where you actually stand relative to it, over and over, with no sense that the ground is holding still long enough to build on.

Cumulative Reasoning as the Alternative

Cumulative reasoning treats reasoning as a trajectory across time rather than a series of disconnected outputs. Each step produces something the next person can actually examine, criticize, and extend — which is precisely what restores orientation: not more information, but a structure that lets someone follow how an idea actually evolved, rather than only ever meeting it as a finished conclusion with no visible history.

Restoring Reasoning Continuity

If interpretive entropy is really behind both the lost orientation and the fatigue it produces, the fix has to be structural, not just more effort or more attention. Infrastructure that preserves reasoning traceability lets ideas accumulate across time instead of resetting with every new conversation. That doesn't mean eliminating disagreement — disagreement is fine, even useful. It means making sure the reasoning behind an idea stays visible for whoever encounters it next, so the next debate can actually start from where the last one left off rather than from zero.

Orientation requires memory. When reasoning loses memory, understanding loses direction.

— Arne Mayoh
