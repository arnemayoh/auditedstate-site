Coherence of Understanding

Why civilization increasingly struggles to preserve meaning across distributed systems

Civilization is increasingly optimizing for operational coherence while losing coherence of understanding. Modern systems are extraordinarily good at preserving operations — data flows, transactions complete, services stay online — and yet more and more people report a strange, hard-to-name condition: things still function, but fewer people actually understand what's happening while they do. Organizations keep operating while shared understanding fragments underneath them. AI systems generate coherent-looking output while the reasoning behind it quietly drifts. We usually reach for words like confusion, complexity, or misalignment to describe this. A more precise word might be incoherence.

What Coherence Originally Meant

Coherence gets used narrowly today — stable phase relationships in physics, synchronization in engineering, consistency across state transitions in computing. But its older, plainer meaning was simply that something holds together intelligibly: it can be followed, it stays understandable across transitions, its parts stay meaningfully related to each other. In linguistics, coherence is what makes sentences add up to meaning rather than random fragments. In organizations, it's whether people still share an interpretable sense of purpose and state. Across every domain it shows up in, the same pattern holds — coherence is the condition where relationships stay stable enough for understanding to persist, and incoherence is what happens when those relationships break down.

Drift Begins at Representation, Not Computation

Even a phrase as simple as "one apple" turns out to be less precise than it looks the moment you actually press on it: Is a bitten apple still one apple? A sliced one? A rotten one? An apple in a legal dispute, or a nutritional ledger, or an image classifier?

The object itself never fully survives being turned into a symbol. "Apple" is a negotiated compression of whatever context it's being used in — which means drift doesn't begin during computation, the way most discussions of AI state transformation quietly assume. It begins during representation itself. "State" is already an interpreted approximation of understanding, not direct access to reality, so systems inherit interpretive drift before any computation even starts. The real challenge isn't preserving state transitions correctly. It's preserving coherence of understanding across interpretations that were already unstable on arrival.

Local Accuracy, Global Incoherence

Once meaning has been compressed into data, systems have to reconstruct it later — and the original context that produced it is no longer fully present to draw on. What's left is inference: of intent, of assumptions, of relational significance that isn't deterministic to recover, only interpretive. That's why a system can be locally accurate and globally incoherent at the same time — each component operating correctly relative to its own narrow slice of representation, while the broader continuity of understanding quietly collapses around it. The practical result is more effort spent reconstructing understanding than accumulating it: reconstruction cost, showing up again from a different angle.

Two Different Kinds of Coherence

Modern infrastructure is excellent at operational coherence — packets arrive, databases synchronize, distributed systems maintain transactional integrity. None of that is the same thing as coherence of understanding, and AI-mediated systems make the gap between them sharper than ever: an AI system can be extraordinarily good at generating locally coherent output, while that output steadily detaches from the intention, context, and assumptions that were supposed to be behind it. The result is a genuinely dangerous illusion — the appearance of understanding, without the continuity that real understanding actually depends on.

Why This Keeps Recurring Across Every Field

Physics encounters coherence in relational stability across fields and waves. Biology encounters it in living coordination. Organizations encounter it in shared operational understanding. Governance encounters it in responsibility and justification. Each field arrived at roughly the same underlying pattern independently: systems fail when relational continuity breaks, not when information runs out. Storage preserves symbols. Logs preserve events. None of that automatically preserves meaning, interpretation, or continuity of understanding — which is a different, harder thing to hold onto than any of them.

What Continuity of Understanding Actually Requires

Continuity of understanding isn't just memory. It's the condition under which reasoning stays reconstructable, transferable, and actionable across time, systems, and people, without unacceptable loss of meaning or responsibility along the way. Break continuity, and coherence degrades. Break coherence, and agency becomes unstable. Break agency, and governance fragments. Civilization already has enormous infrastructure for preserving other kinds of continuity — roads, power grids, financial systems, legal institutions. What it hasn't yet learned to preserve structurally is continuity of understanding itself, and as reasoning spreads across humans, organizations, AI systems, and automated decision chains together, understanding can no longer be assumed to stay local or reconstructable on its own. That's the deeper argument behind moving from an Information Superhighway, built to move data, toward a Cognitive Super Highway, built to keep understanding coherent while it moves.

This is, plainly stated, the case for what this research programme names Navigational Coherence — not a separate proposal sitting alongside everything above, but the name for the problem-space this whole essay has just described from the inside.

A system can keep running long after the understanding behind it has quietly stopped holding together.

— Arne Mayoh
