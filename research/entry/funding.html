Funding & Impact

Assessing this work for funding usually means answering a narrower question than assessing it for citation: not only is this rigorous, but does it matter enough, to enough people, soon enough, to be worth backing — the institutional expression of what this research programme calls Navigational Coherence applied to its own case for support.

The Stakes, Stated Plainly

Governance and AI-safety funding increasingly targets disclosure, transparency, and alignment mechanisms. Those matter. But disclosure and alignment both assume something upstream of them: that the reasoning behind a decision remains recoverable at all, by anyone, once the decision has been made.

This programme's claim is that the assumption is already failing, independently of any single AI system's behavior — and that the gap is structural, not merely procedural.

The Research Behind the Claim

The claim is argued, not merely asserted, across two anchor papers:

  • Legible Risk: Why Disclosure Isn't Enough — disclosure and legibility are shown to be different properties; a disclosed risk that cannot be traced to a stable object or a responsible party remains invisible in practice.
  • Super-Optimization: When Optimization Outpaces the Capacity for Double-Loop Revision — the underlying mechanism does not require artificial intelligence at all; it is already visible in corporations and bureaucracies, and AI accelerates it rather than originating it.

These sit inside a wider, sustained research programme — over twenty further papers developing adjacent claims (Interpretive Entropy, the Continuity Threshold, Recoverability, and related mechanisms), not a single pitch built around one idea.

Policy Relevance

The EU AI Act is a serious attempt to govern this problem through transparency, oversight, and accountability requirements. This programme's position is not opposed to that effort — it names the gap regulation cannot close on its own, because part of the problem is structural and navigational rather than procedural.

Not a Concept Awaiting Implementation

The architecture this programme argues for is not a proposal waiting to be built. Over roughly a year, it was developed and refined into a working, multi-user system: agent-agnostic by design, with its own execution, audit, and state-management layers, and arranged across distinct deployment contexts — personal, professional, educational, and institutional — under a shared custodial core.

At that point the open question was no longer whether the architecture worked. It was whether to release it immediately or to first build the governance structure capable of holding it responsibly — the same custodianship question addressed below. The second path was chosen. That has taken a further year.

What funding would support now is not invention. It is the empirical validation this kind of architecture needs before wider claims about it can be responsibly made, and completion of the custodianship structure that keeps it from being released without one.

Where This Stands

This is independent research. The intention is for the architecture's governance — decisions about how the shared infrastructure is stewarded, and by whom — to sit with a nonprofit structure, kept separate from any commercial instantiation built on top of it, so that custodianship of the architecture is not captured by growth or commercial incentives. That structure does not yet formally exist; the intention is stated here plainly, as intent, so a funder can see the direction rather than infer it.

What Support Would Actually Fund

Concretely: continued independent research (the papers above are a programme, not a finished output), and development of the reasoning infrastructure the research argues for — structured reasoning artifacts, decision lineage, and the architectural mechanisms described under Architecture.
