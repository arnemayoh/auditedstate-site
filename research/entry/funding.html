Funding & Impact

For a research funder, the question is not only whether an argument is rigorous. It is whether answering the research question could make a meaningful difference in the world. Audited State investigates a question that reaches across organisations, education, technology and governance: how can people retain the understanding needed to navigate change coherently?

The Stakes: The Human Ability to Navigate Change

Change is unavoidable. Technologies develop, people leave or assume new roles, organisations reorganise, and yesterday's workable assumptions become questionable. Navigating such changes requires more than access to facts or instructions. People need to understand why things were done, recognise when those reasons still apply, revise them when circumstances change, and carry what they learn into the next decision.

The concern is not that every transfer fails, or that understanding is in general decline; neither has been established by this programme. The concern is that we may be improving our ability to store and deliver information faster than we understand the conditions under which recipients can actually make use of it, question it, and build upon it. The programme calls this capacity Navigational Coherence.

The Faultline: From Available Knowledge to Continuing Understanding

Availability → Transfer → Understanding → Continuation

  • Availability: information, procedures, experience or explanations exist and may be accessible.
  • Transfer: another person encounters them through documents, conversation, demonstration, training or an AI-generated response.
  • Understanding: that person can make appropriate use of what was received, explain or demonstrate why it matters, and recognise its limits.
  • Continuation: the understanding can be revisited, challenged, shared with others, corrected and developed as conditions change.

None of these transitions is guaranteed by the one before it. Nor does understanding always take the form of a verbal explanation: practical expertise and experience can carry judgment that is difficult to articulate. The research asks what helps or obstructs these transitions, rather than assuming that more documentation or better retrieval is sufficient.

Why This Matters in Practice

Consider a small business passing from one generation to another. Its contracts, procedures and accounts may be transferred successfully, while the successor is still learning which decisions were based on temporary constraints, personal relationships or hard-won practical experience. The goal is not to preserve every inherited practice. It is to retain enough understanding to judge what should continue and what should change.

AI introduces a related possibility. It can make explanations and answers far easier to obtain, sometimes improving human learning and sometimes allowing good immediate performance without equivalent independent understanding. The distinction matters wherever people must judge an answer, deal with exceptions, train a colleague, or adapt once the original helper or expert is unavailable.

The same question appears in organisational handovers, leadership changes, engineering projects and institutional memory. Safety, accountability and resilience are important consequences, but they are not the whole problem. The wider stake is the ability of people and organisations to keep learning and acting coherently through change.

What Existing Research Establishes — and What It Does Not

Research in organisational learning has measured depreciation of some experience-based performance gains and limits to transfer between units. Learning research distinguishes successful performance with assistance from what people retain and can apply independently. These findings give the question an empirical basis.

They do not establish that human understanding is universally declining, that the different findings share a single cause, or that any proposed continuity technology improves outcomes. Those are matters to investigate. The programme's research bibliography offers a map of related empirical and theoretical literatures; it is not, by itself, a systematic evidence review.

A Proposed Empirical Starting Point

One proposed setting is generational succession in Danish small and medium-sized enterprises, including firms before, during and after a transition. It makes questions about inherited expertise, changed authority, organisational learning and adaptation unusually concrete. The setting is an entry point for studying a broader phenomenon, not the limit of the programme's scope.

An exploratory study could compare existing working practices, ordinary AI assistance with its normal useful capabilities, and AI supported by a shared reasoning framework. An independent evaluator would help determine whether people can reconstruct earlier reasoning, work with unfamiliar situations, pass understanding to colleagues, and return to an inquiry later. Measures would also include time, burden, disagreements and possible harms. Positive, null and adverse findings would all be relevant.

What Already Exists — and What Remains Unproven

The programme includes a privately developed, working, agent-agnostic continuity architecture. A shared reasoning substrate informed by this work is a candidate intervention for empirical research. The question is not simply whether software can preserve records, but whether structured support helps people develop, share and continue understanding.

A working implementation is not independent validation of its benefits. Nor does it establish that the approach is better than ordinary AI, good documentation or skilled human collaboration. A credible evaluation must be able to find that it adds little, helps only in some contexts, or introduces new burdens.

What Funding Would Enable

The immediate need is not a broad claim of proven impact or a commitment to scale a proprietary product. It is a bounded programme of independent, public-benefit investigation, including:

  • Research partnerships and participating organisations with real handover or adaptation questions.
  • Operational measures that distinguish access, communication, usable understanding and continued independent judgment.
  • Fair comparisons of existing practice, ordinary AI and structured continuity support, including delayed and unfamiliar tasks where feasible.
  • Independent evaluation, appropriate participant and data safeguards, and publication of favourable or unfavourable results.
  • Practical, transferable findings and methods that other organisations can use, regardless of which technology is tested.

What Loss Might Cost — and What Continuity Might Make Possible

When people must repeatedly reconstruct understanding, the cost may be more than the effort spent doing so. It may include the progress they could have made instead. The programme calls such foregone possibilities unattained gains.

This is a proposed economic interpretation, not a measured aggregate loss. Research would have to establish when reconstruction occurs, what it costs, and whether continuity support changes the outcome.

Research Foundations

This proposal sits within a wider body of circulated research papers concerning interpretive change, continuity, recoverability, decision lineage and human agency. The papers advance arguments and candidate mechanisms; they should not be mistaken for independent evidence that the proposed intervention works.

Among the related strands are Succession Over Accumulation (on feedforward and continuity), Persistent Semantic Scaffolds and the Pay-It-Forward Record (on reconstructing reasoning), and Legible Risk (on the limits of disclosure). They approach related questions from different directions.

Governance, Policy & Safety

Continuity of understanding also matters for transparent decisions, effective oversight, safety and repair. A formally disclosed decision is not necessarily one that another person can understand or challenge. These concerns remain part of the programme, but are downstream of its broader interest in human navigational capacity.

→ Resilience & Safety and EU AI Act structural alignment examine those implications. The proposed mechanisms are not independently demonstrated guarantees or legal advice.

The long-term intention is for shared research infrastructure to be stewarded independently of any commercial implementation, with safeguards for research autonomy, publication and continuity. An independent nonprofit custodian is a possible outcome, not an organisation already established or a prerequisite that has silently been met. The near-term task is to determine whether the research merits broader development and what governance it would require.

Where This Stands

The research programme and a candidate implementation exist. The proposed comparative pilot has not yet been independently evaluated, and research partners and participating businesses are not presented here as committed. That distinction is intentional: what is being sought is support for a credible test, not funding on the assumption that the result is already known.
