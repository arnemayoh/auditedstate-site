Points of View

Where the words come from, and what they're standing in for

"Understanding" is a word everyone already knows — the way everyone knows what a cloud is. Point at one and everybody agrees. Look closer, though, and the edges dissolve into fog: is it the water droplets, the shape, the moment you stopped calling it mist? Every field that studies understanding cuts out a piece small enough to work with — attention span, cognitive process, brain function, a statistic. Each piece is real. None of them is the cloud.

Navigational Coherence doesn't try to cut a smaller, cleaner piece. It tries to hold as much of the cloud together as the pieces will allow — including how understanding changes, not just what it is at a single moment.

The starting point isn't a definition of understanding. It's simpler, and harder to argue with: when there is no understanding, there is incoherence. You don't have to agree on what understanding fundamentally is to notice its absence.

One Problem, Many Fires

Funny enough, the moment you try to talk about understanding, you discover the fog doesn't just blur the thing — it blurs the audience too.

An engineer wants a mechanism. A lawyer wants a boundary. A philosopher wants to know if any of this is even true. Someone who just wants to feel less alone in a confusing world wants a story.

None of them are wrong. None of them are asking about a different problem. They're standing at different points around the same fire, and the fire looks different from where each of them is standing.

So this page doesn't try to find the one sentence that reaches everyone. It offers several doors instead. Take the one that opens.
