The Word That Was Already Taken

Why some ideas need a new name, and some just need a warning label

Ask a chemist what's in your shampoo and they'll tell you: chemicals. Ask the bottle, and it'll proudly tell you there are none — "chemical-free," right there on the label. Both statements can't be true. Water is a chemical. Salt is a chemical. The chemist and the marketer are using the same word to mean almost opposite things, and neither one is lying — they're just standing in different rooms that happen to share a doorway.

Same thing happened to "theory." In science, a theory is one of the strongest words available — evolution, gravity, germs, all "just theories," in the sense of being extensively tested explanatory frameworks that keep surviving every attempt to break them. Outside a lab, "it's just a theory" means the opposite: a guess, barely worth taking seriously. Same word, same spelling, nearly opposite jobs.

Neither of these is really anyone's fault. Words don't get assigned by a committee that checks for collisions first. They drift into rooms, pick up whatever a particular field needs them to mean, and nobody sends out a memo when two rooms end up using the same word for different things.

Which is the actual, unglamorous reason this site keeps minting new terms instead of just reaching for familiar ones. It's not to sound impressive. It's because some rooms were already occupied, and walking in anyway doesn't make you understood faster — it makes you confidently misunderstood, which is worse than not being understood at all. "Reconstruction Cost" already has a room: insurance and real estate have used it for decades to mean what it costs to rebuild a damaged building. Reach for that phrase to describe what's lost when institutional knowledge has to be rebuilt from scratch, and a reader doesn't end up slightly confused — they end up picturing a construction estimate, confidently, while missing the actual point entirely. "Shadow AI" already has a room too: enterprise IT uses it for employees quietly using unapproved software. Close enough to cause real static, not close enough to just borrow.

A blank look is an honest response to an unfamiliar word. A confident, wrong answer is what you get from a familiar one pointed at the wrong thing. The second failure is quieter and does more damage, because nobody notices it's happened.

But minting something new isn't automatically the right fix, either — sometimes the room is already occupied by something close enough that the honest move is just to say so out loud, rather than evict it. That's the other half of the same discipline: check whether the word is already spoken for, and pick the cheapest fix that's still honest — a new name where the meanings would collide badly, one clarifying sentence where they're just neighbors.

That sorting work — word by word, checking what's already occupied before deciding whether to build new or just add a sign — is happening, visibly, on the site's own dictionary.
